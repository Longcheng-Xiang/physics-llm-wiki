## Ewart TeX — Central Field Definition and Perturbative Hierarchy (lines 348–358)

> To recap, we have lumped together the Coulomb attraction of the $Z$ protons in the nucleus with the centrally acting part of the mutual electrostatic repulsion of the electrons into $U(r)$. This Central Field goes like $1/r$ at large $r$ and as $Z/r$ at small $r$.
>
> ...the Hamiltonian for the Schrödinger equation will be
> $$\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{0}+\hat{H}_{1}+\hat{H}_{2}+\ldots$$
> $\hat{H}_{1}$ will be the perturbation due to residual electrostatic interactions, $\hat{H}_{2}$ that due to spin-orbit interactions. ... The Central Field Approximation allows us to find solutions of the Schrödinger equation in terms of wave functions of the individual electrons:
> $$\psi\left(n, l, m_{l}, m_{s}\right)$$

## Ewart TeX — Configurations (line 514)

> The energy level will depend on the degree to which the electron penetrates the core of inner electrons, and this depends on $l$. Therefore the energy levels are no longer degenerate in $l$. ... We can therefore specify the energy of the whole system by the energy of the electrons determined by quantum numbers $n$ and $l$. Specifying $n,l$ for each electron gives the electron configurations: $nl^x$ where $x$ indicates the number of electrons with a given $nl$.

## Ewart TeX — Pauli and Least Energy (lines 508–512)

> The energy level-structure for a multi-electron atom is governed by two important principles:
> a) Pauli's Exclusion Principle and
> b) Principle of Least Energy
>
> Pauli forbids all the electrons going into the lowest or ground energy field. The Least Energy principle requires that the lowest energy levels will be filled first.

## Ewart TeX — 4s/3d Crossover at K (lines 535–542)

> At this point things get a little more complicated. We expect the next electron to go into the 3d sub-shell. ... a 3d electron is very like a 3d electron in hydrogen: it spends most of its time in a circular orbit outside the inner shell electrons. An electron in a 4s state however goes relatively close to the nucleus, inside the core, and so ends up more tightly bound — i.e. lower energy — than the 3d electron. So the next element, Potassium, has the configuration
> K: $1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^6\,3s^2\,3p^6\;4s$

## Ewart TeX — Rare Gas Stability (lines 562–572)

> Rare gases ... are chemically inert and have high ionization potentials. ... They all do have filled s and p sub-shells.
> ...
> [Filling a sub-shell] leads to a spherically symmetric charge distribution. Since electrons are indistinguishable all the electrons take on a common wave function. The point is that this results in a higher binding energy for each one of the electrons. So it is harder for them to lose an electron in a chemical bond — they are chemically inert and have high ionization energies.

## Ewart TeX — Alkali Low Ionization Energy (line 574)

> Alkalis ... have one electron outside the full (sp) sub-shells. This outer, or valence, electron therefore moves in a hydrogen-like central potential. The electron is generally well-screened by ($Z-1$) inner electrons from the nucleus and is easily lost to a chemical bond. They are chemically reactive and have low ionization energies which don't change much from one alkali to another.
